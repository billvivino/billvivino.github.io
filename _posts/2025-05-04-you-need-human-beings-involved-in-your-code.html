You Need Human Beings “Involved” in Your Code

May 3, 2025

🎧 Listen to this article: Play the original 2025 recording

The recording preserves the article as originally published. The text below has been updated to make its original point clearer.

Introduction

When I wrote this, I was already using AI throughout my work. I used it to help draft and polish this article, and Grammarly AI proofread it. My objection was never to AI-assisted development.

I was reacting to public predictions from Dario Amodei and other technology leaders that generating more code would make experienced developers unnecessary. AI expands what people can do. It does not remove the need for a person to understand, direct, verify, and ultimately own the system.

The Hands‑Off Coding Myth

AI can now help a founder build substantial software, not merely a toy or a few snippets. That is real progress. But generating a system and taking responsibility for one are different jobs.

Someone still has to understand why each piece exists, how it interacts with the rest of the product, and what happens when requirements, users, data, or infrastructure change. The more quickly AI produces code, the more important that ownership becomes.

Marie Kondo Your Codebase

Never pile things you do not understand on top of more things you do not understand.

A healthy codebase needs the same kind of deliberate attention as an organized room: keep what belongs, remove what does not, and know where every important thing lives.

You Have to Touch the Mess

Cleaning a room still requires decisions. What should stay? What should be discarded? What belongs together? Software requires the same involvement.

AI can propose those decisions and carry them out quickly. A person still has to decide whether they are right for this product, this team, and this moment.

Simplify, simplify, simplify. Discard, discard, discard. The goal is not to reject generated code. The goal is to keep the system understandable as its output accelerates.

AI Can Add Clutter Too

I have seen duplicated views, redundant view models, extraneous logic, and features that overlap because nobody stopped to reconcile the generated changes with the whole system.

That is not an argument against AI. Human developers create clutter too. It is an argument for active technical ownership, especially when the tools can produce changes faster than a team can understand them.

Why AI Won’t Replace Engineers

AI is table stakes. It gives everyone more power, but it does not flatten the difference between novice and expert. A founder with AI can build far more than before. A senior engineer with AI can apply years of architecture, debugging, and production judgment at far greater scale.

The tool raises both baselines; it does not make those baselines equal. Access to the same model does not instantly transfer the experience of the person directing it.

Generation Is Not Management

Modern AI can plan, generate, review, test, and revise surprisingly large parts of a codebase. That makes it a serious engineering tool, not merely a boilerplate generator.

But a tool does not carry the business consequences of its decisions. A human still has to choose the architecture, reconcile competing goals, recognize when the generated approach is wrong, and take responsibility for the result.

This is true beyond software. AI gives lawyers, doctors, architects, analysts, surveyors, and other professionals more leverage too. Software engineering is not uniquely replaceable simply because some of its work can be generated. Across knowledge professions, expertise shapes how safely and effectively that leverage is used.

The Details Still Bite

You do not need a computer science degree to build with AI. You do need to recognize that every builder has blind spots, and inexperienced builders will not always know which generated decisions deserve scrutiny.

A subtle identity or state-management mistake can reset navigation only after an asynchronous fetch. A permissions shortcut can work in development while exposing customer data in production. A plausible schema can become painful once real workflows depend on it.

AI can help diagnose all of these problems. Experience helps a person form the right hypotheses, evaluate the answer, and understand what else a fix might disturb.

I have also watched an AI session circle a bug and add layers of fixes because the relevant context was missing. The answer was not to stop using AI. It was to bring deeper system knowledge into the loop.

What Happens When a Product Keeps Growing

I have had clients take AI-assisted development fully in-house, believing they can keep adding features indefinitely while also handling every other demand of running a business. That can work for a while, and it may be the right choice for an early product.

But continued code generation is not the same as continued system health. Inexperienced decisions accumulate in architecture, data models, permissions, state management, integrations, testing, deployment, and operations.

AI can accelerate those decisions. It does not make their consequences disappear. If the product continues to grow and matter, senior engineering judgment eventually has to enter the system—whether the founder develops that expertise, hires it internally, or brings in outside help again.

Experience Is Still Relative

AI has changed how much one person can accomplish. It has not made every person equally prepared for every decision. A founder with AI may no longer need the same amount of paid engineering to validate an idea. A senior engineer using AI is also faster and able to reason across more of the system.

That relative difference matters most when the software becomes valuable, interconnected, regulated, or difficult to reverse. The point is not that founders cannot build. They clearly can. The point is that capability and accountability remain human, even when AI multiplies both.

Human Beings Still Own the Outcome

The useful question is not whether AI writes the code. It is who understands the system, who verifies the decisions, and who is accountable when reality changes. AI gives that person extraordinary leverage. It does not eliminate the need for the person.
